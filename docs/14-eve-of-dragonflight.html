XIVThe table is set

The Eve of Dragonflight

The Isles awaken, the dracthyr open their eyes, the Storm-Eater breaks her chains — and five mortal dragons fly home

For the first time in the game's history, no apocalypse is scheduled. The Legion is destroyed, the Old Gods are dead or silent, the Jailer is unmade, the faction war is signed away. Into that unprecedented quiet, a beacon older than any of it begins to burn.

1 — The Isles awaken

The Dragon Isles — dormant and hidden since the flights withdrew in Chapter III — flare back to life, and every dragon on Azeroth feels the call. Alexstrasza summons the flights home to rekindle their ancient oaths at the Isles' waygates and oathstones — and, perhaps, to seek what the Dragon Soul took from them: their Aspect-hood itself. Horde and Alliance, at peace for once, each send a grand expedition alongside them. That reawakening has a cause, not just a mood: with the titans' order-magic across the world disturbed and the world-soul's own blood spilled in Silithus, old seals everywhere are weakening. Including one in particular.

2 — The dracthyr open their eyes

On the Forbidden Reach, the dracthyr — Neltharion's hidden soldiers, in stasis since the War of the Scaleborn — wake to a world twenty thousand years older than the one they fell asleep in. Their creator is millennia dead, and history remembers him as its greatest traitor. Two of their commanders, Emberthal and Azurathel, lead their weyrns out into a present that owes them nothing — one toward the Horde, one toward the Alliance. (This is the expansion's new playable race, the Evoker, and their identity crisis is the story's quietest, saddest thread: what do you do when your whole purpose was a dead tyrant's secret?)

3 — The Storm-Eater breaks free

Their waking is no accident. Elemental cultists — modern Primalists, heirs of the old refusal — have already freed Raszageth from the Froststone Vault — a storm given wings, slate scales crawling with lightning, her laugh like splitting sky. The Storm-Eater's goals are exactly what they were twenty thousand years ago, with twenty thousand years of interest: shatter the ordered flights, free her three siblings from the Vault of the Incarnates, and scour the titans' fingerprints from the world. And her old argument has never been harder to answer — the gods whose gift defined the Aspects are gone, the gift itself is spent, and "why did you let them rewrite what we are?" now lands on five mortals who privately wonder the same thing.

4 — The wounds they carry home

  • Alexstrasza returns as a queen without a crown's power, carrying every failure of her long watch — Neltharion's fall, Malygos's execution, the flights' dwindling eggs — and a specific, personal ache: it was her mercy that left the Incarnates alive to return.
  • Nozdormu knows, and has known for ages, a prophecy that he will one day fall and become Murozond, the dragon of the end of time — and must guard the timeline anyway, a warden serving a sentence that ends in becoming the thing he guards against.
  • Kalecgos leads a blue flight reduced to a handful of scholars, heir to a madman's legacy and a nearly empty sky.
  • Merithra must grow into her dead mother Ysera's place as the green flight's leader — an Aspect-shaped hole she never asked to fill.
  • The black flight has no Aspect at all — only rival heirs. Wrathion — in mortal guise a sharp-dressed young man with ember-red eyes and a schemer's smile — the purified-from-the-egg prince who once helped save the world and once catastrophically misjudged it; and Sabellian, Deathwing's scarred, imperious elder son, returned after decades guarding his brood on Outland. Between them stands Ebyssian — who has spent millennia disguised as a black-furred, moose-antlered tauren spiritwalker — an uncorrupted black dragon wanting the job least of anyone, which, in this story, is a qualification. Underneath the succession fight is the deeper excavation the expansion promises: how, exactly, the Earth-Warder fell, and what he left buried.

5 — What kind of story this is

Dragonflight is a deliberate change of key: no scheduled doomsday, no cosmic jailer — a warmer, slower expansion about renewal, inheritance, and what a guardian is for when the gods who commissioned her are gone. It is also, structurally, Blizzard's turn toward continuous, character-forward storytelling, a direction that carries into everything after.

Spoilers: where Dragonflight's own story goes (open only if you want the road ahead)

The expansion's three raid-arcs: Raszageth succeeds in freeing her siblings at the cost of her own life (Vault of the Incarnates); Neltharion's buried laboratory Aberrus is opened, revealing the depth of his experiments and handing dangerous inheritance to the black flight's heirs while Iridikron seeks a darker prize (Aberrus, the Shadowed Crucible); and Fyrakk, empowered by shadowflame, assaults the Emerald Dream to burn the newly-growing World Tree Amirdrassil — dying at its heart, while Vyranoth, moved at last by Alexstrasza's stubborn friendship, breaks with the Incarnates (Amirdrassil, the Dream's Hope). Along the way Tyr is restored, the aspects are re-blessed — by Azeroth herself, not the titans, which quietly answers the Incarnates' old accusation — and Iridikron departs, essence of Galakrond in hand, to serve a new master: the harbinger Xal'atath, setting up the Worldsoul Saga.

That saga is the game's current era: The War Within (2024) took the fight beneath the world toward the world-soul herself; Midnight (March 2026) brings the Void's armies to the Sunwell and Quel'Thalas; and The Last Titan will close the trilogy in Northrend. In other words: the questions raised in Chapters I and II of this site — who gets to shape the sleeping god, and what the titans really intended — are finally, openly, the main plot.
