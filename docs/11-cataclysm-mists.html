XICataclysm & Mists of Pandaria

The World Broken, the World Found

Deathwing shatters Azeroth and the Aspects pay everything to stop him; then a hidden continent pays for the factions' war

Two expansions, one arc: the last Old-God-driven apocalypse of the old world, and the self-inflicted catastrophe that followed it. For Dragonflight, this is the most essential chapter on the site.

The Cataclysm

Deep beneath the earth, Deathwing — his body so ruined and so overfull of magma that it could no longer hold itself together, riveted into plates of elementium armor with molten rock glowing through every seam, his jaw sheathed in metal — erupts through the world's crust, driven by the Old Gods' final design. The event is simply called the Cataclysm: coastlines drown, zones burn, the map of the classic world is literally redrawn, and the Elemental Lords Ragnaros (restored) and Al'Akir join his Twilight's Hammer doomsday cult. The Old Gods' plan is explicit now: break the world's order utterly and drown the world-soul in shadow — the "Hour of Twilight."

Against him stand the last four Aspects and an unlikely fifth: Thrall, who has handed the Horde to a new warchief so he can serve the whole world as a shaman. The counterstroke turns on Chapter IV's cursed artifact: Nozdormu guides heroes through time to steal the Dragon Soul from the past — the one weapon bearing the essence of all flights but Deathwing's own. In the Dragon Soul raid, Thrall channels the combined, freely-given power of Alexstrasza, Ysera, Nozdormu, and Malygos's successor Kalecgos through the artifact, and Deathwing is unmade above the Maelstrom — the wound of ten thousand years finally closed.

The price is the premise of Dragonflight. In that final channeling, the Aspects spend the last of their titan-given power and become, in their own word, mortal. Nozdormu names it the Age of Mortals: the dragons' great charge is fulfilled, their purpose spent, their flights barely able to produce another generation. Quietly, over the following years, dragonkind fades from the world's stage.

Mists of Pandaria

With the apocalypse cancelled, the factions immediately resume their favorite pastime. A naval skirmish reveals a continent hidden behind mists since before the Sundering: Pandaria — jade forests, terraced emerald valleys, cloud-wrapped peaks, a continent like a landscape painting — serene homeland of the pandaren, black-and-white bear-folk of unhurried good humor. And prison of the Sha: the lingering breath of dead Y'Shaarj from Chapter II, ink-dark creatures with pale mask-like faces that condense out of doubt, despair, anger, and fear. Into this landscape of emotional landmines the Horde and Alliance import a war, and the land itself sickens around them. It is the setting's most elegant thesis statement: the faction conflict is not colorful sports rivalry; it is a corruption vector.

Its proof is Garrosh Hellscream — Grommash's son, mahogany-brown like the uncorrupted clans, built like a siege engine, his father's black jaw-tattoo and screaming axe both inherited; Thrall's chosen successor, and a warrior who mistakes cruelty for strength. His escalations run from bombing the Alliance city of Theramore off the map to, at last, digging up the sealed heart of Y'Shaarj itself and unleashing it in Pandaria's holiest vale, scarring it perhaps forever. The Horde's own component peoples — trolls under Vol'jin, tauren, blood elves, Forsaken — rise against him, and in the Siege of Orgrimmar a joint Horde-Alliance force drags the warchief from his bunker. Vol'jin becomes warchief; Varian, at the peak of his kingship, chooses peace over conquest; and Garrosh is taken away to stand trial for war crimes. He never serves the sentence — which is how the next chapter starts.
