IIThe primordial age

The Shaping of Azeroth

The Black Empire, the war of stone against shadow, and the wound that became the Well of Eternity

Before the titans found her, Azeroth was a roiling world of primordial elements — and then the Old Gods fell from the sky.

The Elemental Lords and the Black Empire

Newborn Azeroth was ruled by warring spirits of fire, water, air, and earth, commanded by four Elemental Lords. When the four Old Gods — Y'Shaarj, N'Zoth, C'Thun, and Yogg-Saron — crashed into the world, they enslaved the elements, raised servant races of insect and faceless horror, and built a continent-spanning nightmare civilization: the Black Empire. Beneath it all, their roots bored down toward the sleeping world-soul, and the whisper-work of corruption began.

The titan-forged war

The Pantheon found Azeroth, understood what was growing inside her, and understood equally that they dared not fight on her surface themselves — beings their size could kill the world-soul by accident. So they forged armies. Legions of titan-forged warriors of stone and iron, led by the keepers — among them Tyr the warrior, Odyn the proud, and their peers — made war on the Black Empire for ages, shattering the Elemental Lords and banishing them into elemental prison-realms.

The death of Y'Shaarj — and the wound it left

Y'Shaarj, mightiest and most vile of the Old Gods, threatened to overwhelm even the keepers. So Aman'Thul broke the Pantheon's own rule: he reached down from the sky himself and tore Y'Shaarj bodily out of the planet's crust, ripping it apart. It was a victory and a catastrophe at once. The Old God's roots had grown so deep that tearing it free gouged a colossal wound in the world, and the arcane lifeblood of the world-soul herself came fountaining up through it. The keepers labored for ages to stabilize and ward the wound, and the becalmed lake of raw power it left behind was given a name that will echo through every chapter that follows: the Well of Eternity.

The lesson was learned in blood: the remaining three Old Gods were buried too deep to remove without killing the patient. So the titan-forged imprisoned N'Zoth, C'Thun, and Yogg-Saron underground in warded vaults, and hoped the wards would hold forever. Hold onto this above all: the Old Gods are never gone, only buried. Half the tragedies in this chronicle grow out of those prisons.

Even dead, Y'Shaarj kept killing. Its dying breath and scattered remains seeped into the land that would one day be called Pandaria, becoming a lingering evil called the Sha — negative emotion given monstrous form. Its preserved heart was sealed in a vault. Both come back to haunt Chapter XI.

Ordering the world

With the Empire broken, the keepers shaped and seeded the world: they raised mountains, calmed seas, empowered the great beast-spirits called Wild Gods, and built machine-cities like Ulduar to watch over the Old Gods' prisons. They also built new servant races of stone and metal to maintain it all — and here the Old Gods got their revenge. From their prisons they seeded a disease into the titan-forged called the curse of flesh, softening metal and stone into skin and bone over generations. The keepers' stone servants degenerated — as the Old Gods intended, to make them mortal and corruptible — into beings of flesh: the ancestors of humans, dwarves, and gnomes. The joke of the setting is that most of its "ordinary" mortal races are broken machines, and the tragedy is that the curse worked exactly as designed.
